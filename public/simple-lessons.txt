2.1|What is data science?|Data science means using data to answer a useful question. A good answer explains what the data show and what they cannot show.|Data: recorded information. Model: a rule that finds patterns or makes predictions.|Start with a question, such as “Which students may need help?”~Check what was measured and whether the records are usable.~Study the patterns, then explain a decision and its limits.|A teacher counts missing assignments by class. This is data science even if no AI model is used. A prediction model would be an extra step, not a requirement.|Do not assume every data-science task needs deep learning. Sometimes a checked table answers the question.|Question first. Method second.|Give a useful data-science task that needs no prediction model.|Count missing assignments by class, check that each row represents one assignment, and report which classes may need a review. A descriptive table can be useful.|How are AI, machine learning and deep learning related?|AI is the broad idea of computer systems carrying out intelligent tasks. Machine learning learns patterns from data. Deep learning is a family of machine-learning methods using neural networks.
2.2|Turn a vague question into a clear one|Before calculating anything, say who you mean, what you will measure, when you will measure it, and why the answer matters.|Population: the people or things you want to describe. Variable: a recorded feature, such as score or logins.|Name the population.~Choose a measurable variable and a time period.~State the decision. If you compare groups, say which groups and use comparable measurements.|Instead of “Are students disengaged?”, ask “Among first-year students, which programmes had the fewest submitted assignments during weeks 1–4, so we can plan support?”|A word such as “engagement” is not yet a measurement. Explain the recorded behaviour you use to represent it.|Who? What? When? Why?|Rewrite “Students do badly” as a question that could be analysed.|For example: Among first-year students this term, what share of recorded course results are below 50 points, and which courses should be reviewed for extra support? This states the population, measure, period and purpose.|Why should two cohorts be compared at the same stage of the term?|A week-4 count and a full-term count cover different opportunities to act. Their difference may come from timing rather than a real difference between cohorts.
2.3|Describe, compare, predict, or explain a cause|These are different kinds of questions. A pattern in past data does not automatically tell us what will happen if we change something.|Association: two measurements vary together. Causal effect: changing one thing changes an outcome. Confounder: a common cause that can affect both.|For a description, report what happened.~For prediction, test a rule on cases it has not learned from.~For a causal claim, ask what evidence shows that the proposed action changes the outcome.|Students with more logins may have higher grades. Better preparation could lead to both more logins and higher grades. The pattern alone does not prove that forcing logins raises grades.|Do not turn “these are related” into “this causes that”. Good prediction also needs testing; correlation alone is not a performance result.|A pattern is not proof of a cause.|Give two possible reasons why logins and grades move together.|Logins may help learning, or better preparation may increase both logins and grades. Reverse direction and other factors may also matter. The observed pattern does not choose among these explanations.|What extra evidence would help assess a causal claim about support?|A well-designed randomised support comparison could help, along with clear outcomes and follow-up. An observational comparison needs strong assumptions about selection and other causes.
2.4|What does one row mean?|One row might be one student, one course registration, or one student in one week. Count rows only after you know what a row represents.|Row unit, or grain: what one row represents. Key: the value or set of values that identifies one row.|Write “one row = …”.~Choose a key that matches that meaning.~Check row count, distinct students and missing expected IDs separately.|A student taking four courses can have four registration rows. A weekly table needs student_id plus week. If courses repeat across terms, a registration key may also need term or attempt.|Do not call 740 registration rows “740 students”. Repeated IDs are not automatically errors.|The row meaning chooses the key.|A table has 12 rows but only 4 student IDs. Does it necessarily contain errors?|No. It might contain three weekly records for each student. Check the row definition and the intended key before removing anything.|Why might student_id plus course_code still fail as a registration key?|The same student might take the same course in another term or attempt. Add the relevant term or attempt field and check uniqueness at that full definition.
2.5|Know what kind of value you have|A number can be a measurement, a label, or an ordered response. Its meaning decides which calculations make sense.|Nominal: categories with no order. Ordinal: ordered categories without guaranteed equal gaps. Numeric: a measured or counted amount.|Use counts for names such as programme.~Keep IDs as text, especially when they start with zero.~For scores or durations, report units. For ratings, remember that ordered levels do not guarantee equal differences in feeling.|The average of student IDs 00101 and 00103 is not useful. A rating of 4 is above 2, but we cannot automatically say it means twice the satisfaction.|Storage as an integer does not make a value a meaningful quantity.|Meaning matters more than the stored data type.|Why is the mean of programme codes 1, 2 and 3 usually meaningless?|The codes name categories. Changing which programme is called 1 changes the mean without changing any real programme information. Counts or proportions are meaningful instead.|Can a mean satisfaction rating ever be reported?|Yes, if the equal-step numerical treatment is stated and suitable for the task. Also show category frequencies, because the same mean can hide very different response patterns.
2.6|Who should the answer describe?|The people you want to describe may differ from the people you could contact and the people who answered.|Target population: everyone the conclusion concerns. Sampling frame: the list used to reach people. Sample: the people actually observed.|Name the target population.~Check who could be included and who was left out.~Count who replied and consider why some did not.|You want all enrolled students, invite only active platform accounts, and receive 300 replies. Students without accounts are missed by the frame; invited students who do not reply are nonrespondents.|A large number of replies does not automatically make the sample representative.|Wanted, reachable and observed are three different groups.|Explain the difference between coverage error and nonresponse.|Coverage error means the contact or selection list leaves out part of the target population. Nonresponse means a person was included or invited but did not provide the measurement.|Why might 10,000 voluntary replies still give a misleading mean?|People with particular experiences may be more likely to reply. A large sample can still overrepresent them. More records do not automatically remove systematic selection.
2.7|Measurements are often imperfect clues|We often measure something easy to record as a clue about something harder to observe. Check how good that clue is and whether using it could harm people.|Proxy: a stand-in measurement. Reliability: consistent measurement. Measurement validity: whether it measures the intended idea.|Say what the real idea is, such as engagement.~Explain what your recorded clue misses or misreads.~Consider privacy, unequal effects and how people could challenge a decision.|Platform minutes can include an idle browser tab. A student may also study offline. The timer could record consistently while giving an incomplete picture of learning.|A feature that predicts well is not automatically fair, appropriate or a direct measure of the idea you care about.|A useful clue is not the whole truth.|How can a measure be reliable but have weak validity?|A login counter may record each login consistently, yet logins do not fully represent learning. Consistency and measuring the right concept are different questions.|Why might a sensitive attribute be used in an audit but not in an individual decision rule?|It can help check whether outcomes differ unfairly across groups. That does not automatically justify using the attribute to make decisions about individual students.
2.8|Join tables without changing the count by accident|A join adds information from another table by matching keys. Extra or missing matches can change the number or meaning of the rows.|Left join: keep all left-side records and add matches. Many-to-one: many left records can match one right record.|Check the keys before joining.~For registrations joined to students, require unique student IDs on the right.~Check the row count and unmatched IDs after joining.|If one student's four registrations each match two copied student records, they become eight joined rows. Four extra rows have appeared even though no new registration happened.|A left join does not always keep the same number of rows. Duplicate right-side keys can multiply matches.|Check keys before; check matches after.|What does validate='many_to_one' catch, and what does it not prove?|It catches duplicate right-side keys that violate the expected relationship. It does not prove every left key matched or that the matched values are accurate.|Why might an inner join change the population you report on?|It removes left records that have no matching right key. If those missing matches are related to programme or outcome, the resulting table may describe a different group.
2.9|Read the Lab 1 pass-rate code|The lab joins student information to course registrations, then compares recorded pass rates. Each pass result still belongs to a registration.|Binary outcome: a value such as 1 for pass and 0 for fail. Denominator: the count you divide by.|Join registrations to the unique student table.~Group by study mode or programme.~For complete 0/1 results, take the mean and multiply by 100. If results are missing, report known and missing counts separately.|Results [1,1,0] have mean 2/3, or 66.7%. With [1,1,0,missing], the observed mean is still 66.7%, but there are four rows and only three known results.|Do not claim the highest recorded programme pass rate proves the best teaching. Preparation, course mix and grading can differ.|A binary mean is a rate at the current row unit.|Why do size and count sometimes give different numbers?|size counts all entries in the group. count counts nonmissing entries. With four rows and one missing result, size is 4 and count is 3.|What limitation should accompany a programme pass-rate comparison?|The groups may differ in preparation, course selection, grading thresholds or missing results. A descriptive difference alone does not measure a teaching effect.
2.10|Make your work repeatable|Someone should be able to understand where your data came from and run your analysis again from the beginning.|Provenance: where data came from and what happened to it. Data dictionary: definitions and rules for each field.|Keep the original data unchanged.~Record the source, units, missing-value rules and cleaning actions.~Restart the notebook and run every cell in order to check that it works without hidden steps.|A notebook may look correct because an old variable is still in memory. Restarting exposes whether the saved code actually creates everything it needs.|A seed can repeat a random example, but it does not prove the sample represents the real population.|Keep the source and show the steps.|Why keep a raw snapshot before cleaning?|It lets you compare changes, correct mistakes and explain how the final data were produced. It also preserves the original evidence for unresolved values.|What does a random seed guarantee, and what does it not guarantee?|With the same algorithm and sequence of draws, it helps reproduce the random example. It does not guarantee accuracy, representative sampling or good model performance.
3.1|Report an average with its context|An average is only useful if the reader knows what was averaged and how many measurements were available.|Valid n: the number of observed values used. Missing count: the eligible measurements not available.|State the population and row unit.~Report the measurement unit and valid n.~Show missing values and choose a summary that fits the distribution and question.|A survey has 1,200 invitees and 916 ratings. Its observed mean uses 916 ratings, and 284 responses are missing. The mean does not automatically describe every invitee.|Do not report only a mean and hide the missing responses or different groups behind it.|Number + unit + people counted + limitation.|Write a careful one-sentence report for 916 ratings from 1,200 invitees with observed mean 4.1.|Among 916 respondents, the mean recorded rating was 4.1 on the stated scale; 284 invitees had no rating, so this does not establish the mean for all 1,200 invitees.|Why look at a plot even after reporting mean and SD?|The same summaries can hide different tails, peaks, gaps or groups. A plot helps reveal structure that matters for the decision.
3.2|Mean, median and mode|These are three ways to describe the centre of data. They answer different questions and react differently to unusual values.|Mean: total divided by observed count. Median: middle after sorting. Mode: most frequent value.|Sort the values for the median.~Use the mean when the total matters.~For a typical value in a long-tailed distribution, consider median and IQR. Use counts and mode for categories.|For [2,3,3,4,18], the mean is 6, the median is 3 and the mode is 3. The large value 18 pulls the mean upward.|The median is not generally total divided by count. Multiplying median by n does not usually recover total workload.|Mean balances; median finds the middle; mode finds the most common.|Which summary would you use for a typical student's highly skewed platform time, and why?|Median with IQR is often suitable because a few very large times have less effect on it. Still report the distribution and missing count.|When would the mean be more useful than the median for workload?|When total observed workload matters: observed total equals valid n times observed mean. Using a larger population n when values are missing adds an assumption.
3.3|How spread out are the numbers?|Standard deviation, or SD, describes how much values spread around the mean. Variance is the step before taking the square root.|Deviation: value minus mean. Variance: average squared deviation under the chosen divisor. SD: square root of variance.|Find the mean and each deviation.~Square the deviations and add them.~For sample variance divide by n−1; for the stated population-style calculation divide by n. Take the square root for SD.|For [2,4,6], the mean is 4. Squared deviations sum to 8. Sample variance is 8/2=4 and sample SD is 2. Population-style SD is √(8/3), about 1.633.|SD has the original units. Variance has squared units. The n−1 correction concerns sample variance, not a promise that every sample SD is exact.|Square, add, divide, square root.|Why does variance have different units from SD?|Deviations are measured in the original unit. Squaring them produces squared units. Taking the square root returns SD to the original unit.|Can you calculate sample SD from one observed value?|Not with the usual n−1 formula: the divisor is zero. You need at least two observations for that sample-SD calculation.
3.4|A complete calculation by hand|Writing the steps makes your answer easier to check and helps you avoid mixing sample and population formulas.|Working: the intermediate calculations that justify your result. Rounding: shortening a number for display.|Use the eight values 35,40,45,50,60,65,70,75.~Their total is 440 and their mean and median are 55.~Their squared deviations add to 1,500. Sample variance is 1,500/7 and sample SD is about 14.639 points.|The deviations are −20,−15,−10,−5,5,10,15,20. Their squares are 400,225,100,25,25,100,225,400. Add these before dividing.|Do not round every intermediate number. Keep precision until the final result and state units.|Show the path, not only the answer.|Why is the median 55 even though no student scored 55?|There are eight sorted values. The median is the average of positions 4 and 5: (50+60)/2=55. A summary need not be an observed value.|How does population-style variance differ for the same eight scores?|It divides the same squared-deviation total by 8, giving 187.5 points squared, rather than dividing by 7 for sample variance.
3.5|Quartiles and percentiles|Quartiles split the ordered data into sections. Different agreed rules can give different quartiles in a small dataset.|Q1: 25th percentile. Q2: median. Q3: 75th percentile. IQR: Q3−Q1.|Sort the values.~Use the quartile rule stated in the question.~Subtract Q1 from Q3 to describe the width of the middle half.|For the eight practice scores, median-of-halves gives Q1=(40+45)/2=42.5 and Q3=(65+70)/2=67.5. IQR is 25. Linear interpolation gives different quartiles.|A difference between software and hand calculations is not automatically an error. Check the convention first.|Sort first. State the rule.|Why do 42.5 and 43.75 both appear as Q1 for the same eight values?|42.5 uses the median of the lower half. 43.75 comes from linear interpolation. They use different finite-sample quartile conventions.|What does an empirical percentile tell you?|It describes the value's position relative to the observed values under a stated tie/rank convention. It does not prove ability, causation or distance from the mean.
3.6|Boxplots, fences and unusual values|A boxplot gives a compact picture of the middle, spread and unusual values. A review flag is a reason to check a record, not a command to delete it.|Fence: calculated review limit. Whisker: most extreme observed value still inside a fence. Range: maximum−minimum.|Find Q1, Q3 and IQR.~Calculate Q1−1.5×IQR and Q3+1.5×IQR.~Find the actual observations inside those limits for the whiskers. Investigate points outside.|If Q1=20 and Q3=24, IQR=4 and fences are 14 and 30. If the largest value inside is 28, the upper whisker is 28, not 30.|A valid score of 99 may be unusual but still correct. A fence does not know the marked exam result.|Fence is a rule; whisker is a recorded value.|What happens to range when you add observations without removing any?|It cannot shrink. It increases if a new value goes beyond an old endpoint; otherwise it stays the same. SD and IQR do not share this rule.|Should every point beyond a fence be deleted?|No. Check source accuracy, measurement rules and the target population. Deleting a valid unusual person can distort who is represented.
3.7|Look at the shape, not just the average|Data can have tails, gaps or several groups. Two datasets can share the same mean and SD but need different decisions.|Skew: a longer tail in one direction. Bimodal: two main peaks. Bin: an interval used by a histogram.|Look for centre, spread, tails and peaks.~Try sensible histogram bin widths.~If there are meaningful groups, inspect their separate distributions and counts.|One class may have most students near the middle and two extremes. Another may split into low and high groups. The same mean and SD would hide their different support needs.|Equal mean and median do not prove a normal distribution. A symmetric two-peak pattern can have both.|The picture can reveal what the summary hides.|Why can changing histogram bins change your impression?|Wide bins may merge two groups; narrow bins may make random bumps look important. The data are unchanged, so compare sensible choices before claiming a pattern.|Why might the same mean and SD lead to different tutoring plans?|A central cluster with two extremes may need targeted individual help. Two large separated groups may need different levels of teaching for many students.
3.8|When the 68–95–99.7 rule is useful|For a roughly normal, bell-shaped distribution, about 68%, 95% and 99.7% lie within one, two and three SD of the mean.|Normal model: a particular bell-shaped distribution. Approximation: a useful estimate, not an exact promise.|Check whether a roughly normal model is reasonable.~Build intervals as mean ± the stated number of SD.~Use “about” and state the model assumption.|Mean 65.9 and SD 10.9 give a two-SD interval from 44.1 to 87.7. Under the normal model, about 95% lies in it.|Do not apply this rule automatically to strongly right-skewed platform minutes or claim that a finite sample must match exactly.|The percentages need the normal-model assumption.|Does finding about 95% inside two SD prove normality?|No. Other distributions or small samples can produce a similar proportion. Check the whole shape rather than using one proportion as proof.|What can you still do if the normal model looks unsuitable?|Describe the actual distribution with plots, suitable summaries and observed proportions. You can calculate SD without claiming normal-model percentages.
3.9|Z-scores: distance from a reference mean|A z-score tells you how many reference SDs a value lies above or below the reference mean. It has no units.|Reference group: the group that supplies the mean and SD. Percentile: position in an ordered distribution.|Choose and name the reference group.~Calculate (value−mean)/SD.~Explain the sign and size. Only convert to a model-based percentile when the model is justified.|A score of 87 with mean 75 and SD 6 gives z=2. It is two SD above that reference mean. Against a different group's mean and SD, its z-score may change.|A positive z-score is not always good, and z alone does not give a percentile. If SD=0, the usual formula is undefined.|Z is distance, not rank.|Can a value be below the mean but high in percentile rank?|Yes. In a strongly right-skewed list, a very large tail value can pull the mean above most observations. A value can then be below the mean but above many observations.|Why can the same score have two different z-scores?|The reference groups can have different means or SDs. Always name the reference rather than treating z as a property of the score alone.
3.10|How to finish Lab 2 well|The lab asks you to calculate, interpret and defend choices using your own generated case. Understanding the result matters more than copying output.|Case code: identifies your generated version. Interpretation: explaining what a number means for the question.|Use the same student ID for your generated case.~Check calculations, quartile rules, plots and reference groups.~Write a recommendation with your own numbers, units, missing count and a specific limitation.|For “typical student use”, median and IQR may help. For “total workload”, use the observed sum or valid n times mean. These questions do not need the same summary.|Do not substitute lecture numbers for your personalised 240-student lab output.|Use your case; explain your choice.|What should you check before submitting the notebook?|Restart and run all cells, use a consistent ID/case code, verify calculations and units, and explain the actual output rather than leaving only charts or numbers.|Why does a useful recommendation need a limitation?|It shows where the evidence ends. For example, observed platform use may miss offline work or missing responses, so the recommendation should not claim to measure all learning.
4.1|Good data depends on the question|A field can be important for one task and irrelevant to another. Judge whether the needed information is complete, sensible and trustworthy.|Completeness: needed data are present. Validity: values follow rules. Accuracy: values match the true source.|Ask which fields the task needs.~Check completeness, validity, consistency, uniqueness, accuracy and timeliness.~Use source evidence for facts that a simple rule cannot verify.|A blank phone prevents a phone call. It does not prevent counting a known programme. A score of 67 passes a 0–100 check but is inaccurate if the exam sheet says 76.|Do not confuse “passes the rule” with “matches reality”. A timestamp also does not prove a phone still works.|Good for which purpose?|Name a concrete check for timeliness.|Check whether a later authoritative record changed the information. For an urgent call, verify that the phone number is still usable; an update date alone is insufficient.|How do consistency and uniqueness differ?|Consistency concerns agreement between representations or related fields. Uniqueness concerns whether the intended key appears only once at the chosen row definition.
4.2|Check the data before changing it|Start by counting and recording what is there. Then make a cleaning plan that explains each change and its evidence.|Audit log: a record of changes and reasons. Roster: the list of expected people or records.|Save the raw export.~Count rows, distinct IDs, absent roster IDs and missing cells.~For each action, record what changed, how much changed, why, and the risk of another choice.|A file can have 14 rows, 12 different students and no absent roster IDs. Extra copies increase rows without increasing people.|Do not add “three cells changed” and “two rows removed” and report five removed rows. The units differ.|Count first. Change with evidence. Keep a log.|What should a useful cleaning-log entry contain?|The issue, affected records or values, number and unit affected, action, and supporting evidence. This lets another person understand and review the change.|Why is a raw snapshot useful even after all checks pass?|The checks may miss a wrong decision. The snapshot preserves the original values and lets you compare or reverse changes without inventing the past.
4.3|Understand a value before converting it|Changing text to a number is not enough. You must know the unit, date convention and approved category meanings.|Parsing: reading text into a structured value. Coercion: replacing an unparseable value with a missing marker.|Check the dictionary and source convention.~Keep raw text before converting.~Record the difference between an original blank and a value that failed to parse.|If a score uses 0–100 points, verified “67%” becomes 67. If a field is a 0–1 proportion, it becomes 0.67. Under day/month/year, 03/04/2026 means 3 April.|Do not merge Bioengineering into Biology because the names look alike. Do not turn a parser failure into zero.|Meaning first; conversion second.|Why keep “seventy” separate from an originally blank score?|The word contains a possible intended value, but the parser did not confirm it. A blank has no recorded measurement. Keeping their origins separate helps source follow-up.|Why can a successfully parsed date still be wrong?|Both 3 April and 4 March are valid dates for 03/04 under different conventions. Successful parsing does not establish which date the source intended.
4.4|Copies, corrections and repeated events|The same ID can appear more than once for several reasons. The correct action depends on what the rows mean.|Copy: an extra record of the same thing. Correction: a verified replacement. Repeated observation: a new week, course or event.|Remove an extra copy only when it is confirmed.~Use a correction only when the source verifies that it replaces the old value.~Keep real repeated events using a key that includes the event, and investigate unresolved conflicts.|R13 is a confirmed copy of R01. R14 is a verified correction of R02. But week-1 and week-2 records for one student are different observations and should both stay.|The latest upload is not always the most accurate version. Its date alone does not prove it should replace the old record.|Ask why the ID repeats.|What should happen if a later score conflicts with an earlier score but neither is verified?|Flag the conflict and check the authoritative student-and-exam source. Do not silently choose the later or higher value as settled truth.|What row definition and key fit weekly assessments?|One row represents one student's assessment in one week, under the stated course/assessment context. Use student_id plus week and any additional course or assessment identifier needed for uniqueness.
4.5|Zero, unknown and not applicable|These three meanings are different. Replacing them all with zero changes the question your numbers answer.|Zero: measured absence of an amount. Missing: amount unknown. Not applicable: the measurement does not belong to this person or situation.|First decide who belongs in the target population.~Keep observed zero values as zero.~Keep unknown amounts missing and count them. Keep people outside the target population separate.|An enrolled student's recorded zero lab hours is a real measurement. An enrolled student's blank hours are unknown. Someone not enrolled is outside a summary about enrolled lab students.|Do not remove enrolled students from the population count just because their hours are unknown.|Zero is known. Missing is unknown. Not applicable is outside this question.|Should an enrolled student with unknown hours belong in the target population?|Yes. They belong in the enrolled population but not in the valid-measurement denominator for an observed mean. Report the missing count.|Why is filling every missing phone with 0 a poor cleaning action?|It turns unknown contact details into a false stored value. Phone numbers are identifiers, not quantities where zero means no use. Keep an appropriate missing marker.
4.6|Why are values missing?|MCAR, MAR and MNAR describe how missingness happens. The observed counts alone do not prove which explanation is true.|MCAR: missingness unrelated to the values. MAR: after using known information, missingness no longer depends on the missing value. MNAR: that dependence remains.|For MCAR, ask whether answers could be lost regardless of group or rating.~For MAR given group, ask whether equally grouped people reply regardless of satisfaction.~For MNAR, ask whether satisfaction still affects replies within a group.|Evening students may reply less often because they have less time. This is the stated MAR story only if, within Evening, satisfied and dissatisfied students have the same reply probability.|Different reply rates do not prove MAR. Equal reply rates do not guarantee unbiased answers.|Explain the mechanism, not just the label.|Give an example where equal response rates still hide bias.|Two groups might each have a 50% response rate, but only satisfied students reply in one group and only dissatisfied students reply in the other. Equal totals can hide different selection.|What follow-up could help investigate nonresponse?|Contact a sample of nonrespondents using another method and ask the same question. Compare their answers and known characteristics; some people may still not respond, so uncertainty remains.
4.7|Missing answers change who we see|A group can make up a smaller share of replies than of invitations. That changes the weights in the pooled observed mean.|Response rate: replies divided by that group's invitations. Reply share: that group's replies divided by all replies.|Calculate each group's missing or response fraction using its own invited count.~Compare each group's invitation share with its reply share.~Pool the observed means using reply counts, then state the missingness limit.|Day: 72/80 replies, mean 4. Evening: 8/20 replies, mean 2. Evening is 20/100=20% of invitees but 8/80=10% of replies. The observed mean is 3.8 from 80 replies.|Do not average 4 and 2 equally to get the observed pooled mean. The groups have different numbers of replies.|Name the denominator every time.|What are the missing fractions in the Day/Evening example?|Day is missing 8/80, or 10%. Evening is missing 12/20, or 60%. The denominators are each group's invited people.|Why does 3.8 not establish the mean for all invited students?|It uses only the 80 observed replies. Nonrespondents may have different satisfaction, and Evening is less represented among replies than among invitees.
4.8|Four ways to handle a missing value|Recovering a source measurement and guessing a replacement are different actions. Choose the action that fits the analysis.|Recover: obtain the verified observation. Impute: fill using an assumption or model. Exclude for analysis: leave out a measurement for a clearly limited calculation.|Recover an exact verified value when available.~Keep irrelevant missing fields missing, without deleting useful records.~If using observed-only analysis, state the population it describes. Label any imputation as an assumption.|Mean-filling [2,4,missing,missing,missing] gives [2,4,3,3,3]. The mean stays 3, but sample SD falls from about 1.414 to 0.707. Only two answers were actually observed.|More numeric cells do not mean more observed evidence. Mean-filling can make the data look less variable.|A replacement is not a new observation.|Why can dropping rows with missing scores harm programme counts?|Programme counts need programme membership, not a score. Removing students for an unrelated missing field changes who is counted and can distort programme sizes.|What should be reported if a demonstration uses mean imputation?|State the replacement rule, how many cells were filled, how many values were actually observed, and the fact that results depend on an assumption. Keep the final registry unfilled when required.
4.9|Check how assumptions change the answer|Sensitivity analysis asks “What if the missing values were different?” Bounds show the smallest and largest results allowed by the known scale.|Sensitivity scenario: a stated assumption to explore. Bound: an extreme possible value given the available information.|Keep the observed total fixed.~Choose an assumed mean for the missing responses.~Add the assumed missing total, divide by everyone eligible, and compare scenarios with the target.|Twelve people reply with mean 4, so the observed total is 48. Eight ratings are missing on a 1–5 scale. The full mean can be as low as (48+8)/20=2.8 or as high as (48+40)/20=4.4.|A hypothetical missing mean of 5 is not a recovered answer. Bounds are not confidence intervals.|If the conclusion changes, say which assumption matters.|Is “the mean definitely meets 3.5” justified by bounds 2.8 to 4.4?|No. Some allowed missing-value scenarios meet the target and others do not. The current observations alone do not settle the all-person target.|What information would narrow these bounds?|Recovering additional reliable responses reduces the unknown total. Credible evidence about nonrespondents can also support narrower assumptions, which should remain explicit.
4.10|Unusual is not the same as wrong|A value can be statistically unusual and still correct. A value inside normal-looking limits can still be a recording error.|Outlier flag: a value worth checking. Invalid value: breaks a documented rule. Accuracy: agreement with the true source.|Use the dictionary to identify impossible or disallowed values.~Use source evidence to check unusual but possible values.~Keep valid members of the target population. Flag unresolved fields without throwing away other useful information.|Age 220 breaks the stated 16–100 rule, but its correct replacement is unknown. A verified age of 47 belongs in the dataset even if an IQR rule flags it.|Deleting valid older students may lower the reported withdrawal rate by changing who is counted, without improving any student's outcome.|Investigate the value; respect the population.|Why should age 220 not automatically become 22?|It is a plausible guess, not verified evidence. The intended age could be something else. Flag it and consult the source while keeping the raw value.|Which withdrawal rate should lead a report about all enrolled students?|The rate for all enrolled students. You can additionally show age-group rates with counts, but excluding verified older students changes the target population.
4.11|The registry: what should remain?|The final registry should have one current record per expected student while keeping unresolved values honestly missing.|Row removed: the entire record leaves the working table. 5.4|Min–max scaling and new values|Min–max scaling uses the training minimum and maximum as a fixed ruler. 5.5|Use logs to shrink large gaps|A log transform compresses large positive differences while keeping the order.
